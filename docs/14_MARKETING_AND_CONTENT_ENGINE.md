**Owner:** Founder (acting CMO) · **Status:** PROPOSED · **Version:** 1.0 (7 September 2026) · **Governed by:** `/FACTS_BASE.md` language rules and `/docs/03_BRAND_SYSTEM.md` voice rules · **Feeds:** `/docs/13_SALES_ENGINE.md` (lead generation), `/assets/CONTENT_CALENDAR.csv`, `/assets/LAUNCH_CAMPAIGN.md`

## Executive view
1. Vytalix has no website, LinkedIn page, content, list or press footprint (VERIFIED absence, FACTS_BASE §1). Marketing therefore starts from zero and must produce sales conversations, not vanity reach: the only marketing KPI that matters in the first 90 days is **qualified conversations booked for the sales engine**.
2. The strategy is founder-led, LinkedIn-first, evidence-led. One channel done daily (LinkedIn) beats six channels done badly. Website, newsletter and one research report are the supporting assets; paid media is not used before Month 6 (ESTIMATE budget < £500/month thereafter).
3. Every piece of content obeys the stage-honest rule: Vytalix is pre-seed and founder-led; MaternaLink is "in development"; no claims of clients, partners, NHS approval or clinical outcomes. Credibility is built by showing thinking, not by claiming traction.
4. Targets for the 90-day launch (PROPOSED, not forecasts): 1,500 relevant LinkedIn followers, 36 founder posts, 3 long-form articles, 1 report published, 1 webinar delivered, 300 newsletter subscribers, 15 inbound conversations, 5 inbound-sourced discovery calls.
5. Cost: £0–£150/month tooling in the first 90 days (ESTIMATE: domain, email, newsletter tool, design tool); £2k–£4k one-off for the report design and any freelance editing (ESTIMATE, optional).

## 7. Ten LinkedIn posts (founder voice; ready to publish; no fake claims)

**Post 1 — Week 1, Tuesday. "Why I'm starting a health-tech group with a consultancy, not an app"**

> I'm starting Vytalix this week. Technology for Life.
>
> Most health-tech companies begin with an app and go looking for revenue. We're doing it the other way round.
>
> Vytalix has four parts: Advisory, Consulting, Health Solutions and E-Learning. The first two exist to pay for the second two. The product — MaternaLink, in development, a maternal care-coordination platform — will take years to validate properly. Services can be useful to someone next month.
>
> That's not a compromise. It's the only honest way I can see to build something that touches maternity care: earn the right to build by working inside the problem first.
>
> We're pre-seed and founder-led. No clients yet, no partners yet. I'll post what we learn as we go, including the things that don't work.
>
> If you run a health-tech company trying to reach the NHS, or a maternity service trying to make digital tools useful, I'd like to talk.

**Post 2 — Week 1, Wednesday. "Day 1: what Vytalix is, what it isn't, and what I don't know yet"**

> Day 1 of building Vytalix in public.
>
> What it is: a UK health-technology group. Advisory and consulting for health-tech founders and health organisations. A product in development for maternity care coordination. Learning content later.
>
> What it isn't: NHS-approved, clinically validated, funded, or a team of twenty. It's me, a plan, and a list of people I need to convince.
>
> What I don't know yet:
> – whether the name survives a trademark search (there are four similar names out there)
> – whether buyers will pay what I think the work is worth
> – whether a maternity unit will co-design with a company this early
>
> I'll report back on all three. The plan is 90 days long and public.

**Post 3 — Week 2, Tuesday. "Ockenden and Kirkup keep finding the same failure: communication"**

> Read the Ockenden report (2022) and the Kirkup report on East Kent (2022) side by side and one pattern repeats: information didn't move.
>
> Between shifts. Between community and hospital. Between the woman and the team. Concerns raised and not acted on. Handovers that lost the thread.
>
> Both reports are public; the findings are theirs, not mine, and worth reading in full before anyone builds anything for maternity.
>
> Here's what I take from them as someone designing a care-coordination tool: the hardest problem is not prediction. It's making sure the right person sees the right thing in time, and that someone owns it.
>
> That's a workflow, ownership and accountability problem. Software can support it. Software can't replace the person who picks up the phone.
>
> We're designing MaternaLink (in development) around that view: display, route, remind, record who owns what. Not diagnose.

**Post 4 — Week 2, Wednesday. "We are calling the AI risk score a research track. Here's why."**

> The original MaternaLink concept from 2025 described a multimodal AI score that would predict complications like pre-eclampsia and sepsis.
>
> It's an interesting research idea. It is not a product we can ship, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> To make a clinical prediction claim you need data you have lawful access to, an ethics-approved study, validation on the population you'll serve, a clinical safety case, and, in the UK, a medical-device pathway with the MHRA. We have none of that today.
>
> So v1 of MaternaLink is a care-coordination and communication platform. Non-diagnostic. It shows information to clinicians; it does not interpret it. The AI work moves to a research track that only advances with partners who can do it properly.
>
> Slower. Less exciting on a slide. Honest.

**Post 5 — Week 3, Tuesday. "What DTAC actually asks for (and what it doesn't)"**

> If you want to sell digital health software to the NHS, someone will ask you about DTAC. Here's the shape of it, in plain terms.
>
> DTAC — Digital Technology Assessment Criteria — covers five things: clinical safety, data protection, technical security, interoperability, and usability/accessibility.
>
> It asks for evidence: a clinical safety case under DCB0129 with a named Clinical Safety Officer; your Data Security and Protection Toolkit status; a DPIA; Cyber Essentials (Plus is stronger); penetration test results; how you handle open standards; and accessibility conformance.
>
> What it doesn't do: it isn't a certification you "get" and put on your website. It's a set of criteria each buying organisation assesses. And it doesn't replace medical-device regulation if your software makes clinical claims.
>
> The founders who cope best treat it as product design input from day one, not a form to fill in at procurement.
>
> Source and detail: NHS England's DTAC page. This is a founder's summary, not legal advice.

**Post 6 — Week 4, Tuesday. "The £300-per-woman question"**

> A number I've been thinking about: £300 per woman per year for a maternal digital-health programme in a Nigerian state.
>
> It's a figure I've seen proposed. Roughly a dollar a day per mother. It sounds reasonable until you compare it with how existing programmes are funded.
>
> The digital maternal programme already running in Ekiti and Lagos is donor-funded (MSD for Mothers, per public reporting from Nigeria Health Watch). Donor-funded per-woman costs for messaging-and-coordination programmes are, by my estimate, a small fraction of £300. I'd want to validate that with people who run those programmes before I quoted anything.
>
> My working rule for pricing anything in maternal health: start from cost-to-serve, add the evidence you're generating, and be able to defend the number in front of the person who signs off the budget and the person who benefits.
>
> If you've priced a programme like this, I'd like to compare notes.

**Post 7 — Week 5, Tuesday. "Ekiti already has a digital maternal programme. That's good news, if you design for it."**

> When we looked at Ekiti State, Nigeria, we found something important: there's already a maternal digital-health programme running there — mDoc's Digital Mom Project, funded by MSD for Mothers, with around 24,000 women onboarded and 500+ providers trained as of January 2026 (Nigeria Health Watch reporting).
>
> A year ago that might have looked like a problem. I think it's the opposite.
>
> It means the state has demand, digital literacy among providers, and a funder who cares. A new entrant that shows up promising to replace all that is going to lose. A new entrant that asks "what's the gap, and how do we connect to what exists?" has a conversation worth having.
>
> So our approach for Nigeria is: partner, complement, or pick a different state — never displace on price. Interoperability with what's there (DHIS2 exports, referral and facility-side coordination) is the design brief.
>
> If you work in this space in Nigeria, tell me what I'm missing.

**Post 8 — Week 6, Tuesday. "Is your app a medical device? The one question that decides it (UK)"**

> Founders ask me this more than anything else. Here's the one question that decides most cases:
>
> Does your software interpret data about an individual for a medical purpose — diagnose, monitor, predict, prognose, treat?
>
> If yes, you're probably in medical-device territory under the UK Medical Devices Regulations 2002, and the MHRA's software-as-a-medical-device guidance applies. Classification, a technical file, and possibly an Approved Body follow.
>
> If no — you schedule, remind, educate, record, display, route messages — you're probably not. But write down why. A documented qualification decision is the thing a buyer, an investor and a regulator will all ask for.
>
> The line moves with features. A threshold alert on a blood-pressure reading crosses it. A "risk score" crosses it. A dashboard that shows the reading to a midwife doesn't.
>
> That's why we wrote MaternaLink's intended-use statement before we wrote code. It's one paragraph. It controls everything.
>
> Founder's summary, not legal advice. Get a regulatory adviser before you commit.

**Post 9 — Week 7, Wednesday. "The IP conversation every founder with a collaborator must have early"**

> Uncomfortable truth from our first weeks: the most important document Vytalix needed wasn't a pitch deck. It was a written agreement about who owns what.
>
> If you've been building something with a collaborator — a co-founder, a developer, a friend who made the first prototype — and there's nothing in writing, you have a problem you can't see yet. Every investor will find it in the first ten minutes of diligence. Every serious customer will ask.
>
> What we're doing: a direct conversation, heads of terms in writing, then a solicitor. Options on the table are the normal ones — assignment for equity, a licence, or a joint venture. What matters is that it's written down and signed before any money, contract or pilot depends on it.
>
> It's awkward. It's also respect: for the work someone did, and for the company you're both trying to build.
>
> If you're in this situation, do it this week.

## 9. Two research report concepts (with methodology)

### Report 1 — "The State of Digital Maternal Care Coordination: UK and Nigeria, 2026" (Month 3; 20–28 pages)
- **Research question:** Where does information fail to move in maternity care, and what do frontline professionals and women say a digital coordination layer must and must not do?
- **Method (mixed, small-n, transparent):** (a) desk review of public inquiries (Ockenden, Kirkup, MBRRACE-UK), NHS maternity digital policy and Nigeria national maternal-health frameworks; (b) 20 semi-structured interviews (10 UK: midwives, obstetricians, maternity digital leads, charity staff; 10 Nigeria: clinicians, programme managers, NGO staff), recruited via LinkedIn and warm intros, 30 minutes each, consented, anonymised; (c) a 15-question online survey (target n ≥ 60; convenience sample, stated as such); (d) landscape scan of 15–20 existing tools with public information only.
- **Ethics and data:** not research involving patients; no patient data; participant information sheet and consent; data stored in Vytalix-controlled workspace; ICO registration in place before survey launch; privacy notice linked. TO VALIDATE whether any NHS staff participation needs local approval (usually not for professional opinion surveys, but confirm).
- **Outputs:** 8–10 findings; a "must / must not" design list; a buyer's checklist; a stated limitations section; open dataset of aggregated survey results.
- **Distribution:** gated download; LinkedIn carousel; PR pitch; sent to all interviewees; used in proposals and investor materials.
- **KPIs:** 150 downloads in 14 days; 400 in 90 days; 3 citations; 5 inbound conversations.

### Report 2 — "Paying for Maternal Digital Health in Africa: cost-to-serve, funders and what governments actually buy" (Month 9; 20–30 pages)
- **Research question:** What does it really cost to serve one enrolled woman per year with a messaging-and-coordination programme, who pays today, and what evidence do funders and ministries require?
- **Method:** (a) public programme documentation and evaluations (mDoc, MomConnect-style programmes, GFF/MSD for Mothers reports; all cited); (b) 15 interviews (donor programme officers, state MoH digital leads, NGO implementers, telecom health leads) in Nigeria, Kenya and one further market; (c) a bottom-up cost model with stated assumptions (SMS/WhatsApp costs, CHW time, hosting, support) published as a spreadsheet; (d) sensitivity analysis.
- **Ethics and data:** professional interviews only; consented; no patient data.
- **Outputs:** cost-per-woman-year ranges by delivery model; funder evidence requirements matrix; a pricing playbook; explicit note that Vytalix's own proposals will follow the report's method.
- **KPIs:** 300 downloads in 90 days; 3 S4 conversations; 1 donor conversation; 1 media mention.
