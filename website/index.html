How we work

Built for care, not for demos

The gap between a good idea and a safe, adopted product is closed by people who can advise, deliver, build and teach. Four rules run through everything we do.

  1. We say what it is. Every proposal, product and course states its stage, scope and limits in the first paragraph.
  2. We will tell you if it should not be built. A discovery sprint that ends in "don't" is a success, and we price it that way.
  3. Fixed scope, fixed price, written change control. No open-ended day-rate drift. Deliverables have acceptance criteria before we start.
  4. Every engagement leaves something reusable. A template, a checklist, a benchmark — anonymised, and fed back into our learning material.
Vytalix Health Solutions

MaternaLink In development

A care-coordination and communication platform for maternity services, designed to help women stay connected to their care team and help care teams hand over safely.

Our first product focus is maternal health, where outcomes are unequal and coordination fails too often. MaternaLink is designed to support communication, education, appointment adherence and handover. It does not diagnose, predict or triage, and it is not a medical device.

An early prototype exists. Everything else — pilots, evidence, assurance — is on a published roadmap with validation gates, not in a press release.

Why maternal health

Outcomes are unequal, and the failure is often one of coordination

MBRRACE-UK's annual Saving Lives, Improving Mothers' Care reports have found, year after year, that Black women in the UK are several times more likely to die during or after pregnancy than White women, and that women in the most deprived areas face roughly double the risk. [Cite the current MBRRACE-UK report and exact figures before publication.]

The Ockenden (2022) and Kirkup (2022) reviews describe the same pattern from the inside: women not listened to, escalation that did not happen, information lost at handover.

In Nigeria, the largest single share of the world's maternal deaths, the dominant failure modes are the "three delays" — deciding to seek care, reaching it, and receiving adequate care once there. [Cite WHO/UNICEF Trends in maternal mortality before publication.]

None of this is solved by software alone. But a woman's own account of how she feels is the most under-used information in maternity care, and handover is where it gets lost. That is the gap we are working on — and we will measure whether we help.
